Product Design · Case Study

Transforming IT request fulfillment

Employees at Blue Harbor had to manually raise a ticket in ServiceNow for almost anything — software access, a broken laptop, a simple question. I designed the Employee Portal Application (EPA): a mobile app that replaces that manual process with an automation-first experience, resolving requests in a tap instead of a form.

*Blue Harbor is a fictional client name used to protect confidentiality.

About

As a Designer at TCS Interactive, I designed the Employee Portal Application — a mobile app that makes IT requests easier for everyone at Blue Harbor. Instead of filling out a form, employees raise requests or report issues through a smart, automation-first flow that can grant access to software or services with a single tap.

Replace the manual ServiceNow ticketing process with a modern, self-serve mobile experience.

Use automation to resolve common requests instantly instead of routing everything to a support queue.

Why Blue Harbor needed this

Ticketing ran entirely through ServiceNow: employees manually created a ticket for every request, and support teams manually fulfilled each one. Ticket volume was resolved almost entirely by hand.

An early automation assessment projected ~$760K in annual savings — making the Employee Portal App pivotal to achieving end-to-end automation of these requests.

Research

To understand the existing ticket-raising journey end to end, I raised several tickets myself through ServiceNow and mapped the pains and gains into a Customer Journey Map — the foundation the interview questions were built on.

Target user — End user

An end user here is any Blue Harbor employee trying to fulfill an IT request — across departments, roles, and technical comfort levels.

Interview

Interviews were conducted to find out the peaks and pits in the experience of raising a ticket through the existing web portal (ServiceNow), and to uncover behavior patterns around ticket raising — what motivated people, and what held them back.

4End users interviewed, across departments and technical comfort levels.

2Admins interviewed, who fulfil and route tickets day to day.

Data Synthesis

Quotes from the interviews were mapped into clusters. Several rounds of clustering surfaced the problem areas below.

Form Filling

Repeated information needs to be filled in while raising different tickets.
Some of the entries are difficult to understand.
The form-filling process is very time consuming.

Ticket Tracking

Difficulty in tracking the status of a raised ticket.
There is no categorization present while tracking a ticket.
The overall view of the tickets is not there.
Receiving multiple emails for a single ticket.

Ticket Raising

No priority is given to important tickets — you have to find it the same way as one that's of no use.
Lot of confusion between "Order Something" and "Report an Issue."
It takes a lot of steps to raise a ticket — raising several at once means wasting hours on it.
The search doesn't return valid results if the ticket name is misspelled.
Some of the inputs on the form are difficult to understand.

Admin

The support team may not always grasp the user's problem, which delays routing the ticket to the right team.
Sometimes users raise a ticket directly without reading its details.
Users rarely check whether they can resolve the issue themselves — most raise a ticket as soon as they hit it.
End users don't fill the form properly, which creates confusion while resolving the ticket.

Lack of Knowledge

No clear categorization between "Order Something" and "Raise an Issue."
Communicating difficulties and needs to the support team is very difficult.
There's no guide to help select the right ticket for an issue.
Tickets get raised even when the wrong one is selected.

What research surfaced

Onboarding felt like a circus

New employees had to submit multiple separate tickets just to request the access they needed on day one.

Finding the right ticket was a scavenger hunt

Even keyword search surfaced too many options, and there was no dashboard to guide people to the right one.

The same form, again and again

Employees repeatedly filled near-identical forms to request access to different software or services.

Many issues were self-resolvable

Most tickets could have been fixed by the user in a few steps — but with no guidance, they raised a ticket instead.

Wrong category, wrong ticket

Users frequently raised the wrong category of ticket after too many steps on ServiceNow.

Towards Solution

Defining the design goals and structuring a solution — five goals shaped every screen that followed.

Discoverability

Search, intuitive navigation and organized content get employees to the right ticket quickly.

Affordance

Quick access to Raised Tickets, My Approvals and Order Something is obvious, not hidden.

Playful

Interactive widgets and small playful touches keep employees engaged with the app.

Personalization

The app adapts to individual roles and preferences, surfacing what's relevant to that person.

Ease of use

Discoverability and affordance only matter if employees can act on them with minimal effort.

Iterations

Several rounds of design were shared with stakeholders, who gave feedback on overall impression, usability, and how difficult each direction would be to implement.

Rethinking bottom navigation

In the old flow, raising a ticket meant first choosing between "Order Something" or "Raise an Issue," then picking from a long list under whichever section — so users frequently ended up selecting the wrong ticket. I iterated heavily on bottom navigation so every core function a user comes to EPA for is one tap away, with first-level categorization built into the nav itself.

Inside the app

A walk through the experience

01

Navigation in motion

Ensuring that employees quickly find what they are looking for, I iterated a lot on bottom navigation. I aimed to make all the functionality a user comes to EPA for available right on the bottom navigation, so they don't need to search other screens for a particular feature.

02

Home Screen

To engage the user with the application, I decided to add this section where they can view global news, location-based news, team birthdays, etc.

The widget is added for the My IT card to make it interactive and eye-catching. Most tickets raised are for hardware device issues or for software expiry or access issues — to make those tickets handy, a My IT section has been added on the home screen.

If any action is needed from the end user side on any of the devices tagged to them, or for their software, the user gets an alert from this section and can directly raise the ticket.

For the Access category of tickets, the user can directly raise the ticket with just a click on the Request Access button.

To make important tickets visible and handy to the user, I have added tabs such as Trending, Raised by Colleague, and Role Specific. The user can see all the important tickets from this section and can instantly raise a ticket.

03

My IT

From the data, it was observed that in most cases users raise a ticket for a device issue or for software they already have access to. Previously these tickets lived inside Order Something and weren't handy, with many touchpoints in the journey — so EPA gives them a completely different, dedicated section. In My IT, the user switches between the Devices (hardware) tagged to them and the Software they have access to. If any action is required from the user, the required-action card is surfaced upfront so it notifies them and they can easily raise the ticket. All devices tagged to the user are shown under Your Devices — if there's an issue with one, the user picks that device, selects the ticket, and raises it. The Software tab has 4 sub-tabs: software the user has access to, trending apps and software, updates, and all company apps installed on the phone — all listed to make them handy for raising an issue.

04

Access

There were many tickets for which the form-filling process could be eliminated entirely — previously users still had to fill a form to raise them. Under Access, every ticket that doesn't need a form is listed; with just one click of the Request Access button, the user can raise it. Multiple similar widgets categorize the tickets so users can easily locate what they need — in Trending Categories, all trending groups are listed as tabs, and under each group the respective trending tickets are listed, scrollable right on the widget so the user can raise a ticket directly.

05

Request & Report Issue

The Request & Report Issue section shows all the tickets that used to live under Order Something and Report an Issue in ServiceNow. The cards and widgets here are categorized the same way as on the Access page, to maintain consistency.

06

Support

Users can easily raise tickets using the support feature. Within Support, a smart chat assists users in finding and submitting tickets — guiding them on the necessary actions to take when encountering issues with any of the company's services, and giving clear instructions on the steps to follow before raising a ticket.

07

Filtering the ticket

I used filters to let users select relevant tickets by reducing data sets to their most manageable, relevant results. There are four stages to filtering a ticket — applying a filter at one stage alters the data of the next. Stage 1 is category selection: users start by selecting the category of products they're interested in. Stage 2 lets them narrow the search further by selecting subcategories, after which the respective tickets become available to select. Users can change the filter from the live screen for better interaction — on selecting a filter, they choose the category from the overlay screen. After the third stage, data for the fourth stage becomes available in groups; a filter isn't provided for the fourth stage, since the data there is very limited.

08

Eliminating the form-filling process

While raising a ticket on ServiceNow, users found the form-filling process very tedious and repetitive — needing to fill the form repeatedly for different tickets, often entering the same information more than once. Admin interviews also found that users could resolve many issues themselves in a few steps, but since they weren't aware of those steps, they raised a ticket instead. A smart-chat-based solution addresses this: after selecting a ticket, the app guides the user through resolving it — and even if that doesn't work, the app still raises the ticket by asking only for the fields that are actually needed.

09

Other supportive screens

Notification, Profile, and Personal Information.

This case study covers the fundamental screens within the limits of space — there are more supporting flows and edge cases in the full product that aren't included here.

Searching the ticket

The search functionality allows the user to quickly locate a specific ticket. Employees can enter keywords, ticket numbers, or other search criteria, and the system promptly displays matching results. The number of results available is displayed — in this example, we can clearly see 6 results, giving the user an idea of how long it will take to go through all of them. The auto-suggestion feature provides real-time, predictive suggestions as users type in the search bar.

What I learned

Complex workflow design

Simplifying an intricate ticketing workflow for users, without losing functionality for admins, was a core skill this project sharpened.

Information architecture

Structuring and organizing information to genuinely improve discoverability — not just reorganize it — took real iteration.

Personalization in UX

Tailoring the experience to individual roles and preferences meant designing adaptive interfaces, not one fixed layout.

Automation-first design turned a manual, form-heavy process into something employees can resolve in a tap — proving that the best ticket is often the one that never needs to be raised.
